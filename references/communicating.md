# Communicating and justifying product decisions

Without support, an idea isn't a good idea. Justifying decisions matters as much as creating the right solution.

## Understand stakeholders first

Use the same empathy questions as for users, aimed at stakeholders (informal is fine — grab a coffee):

- 💛 What's their vision for the future of the business?
- ☔️ What's the biggest challenge they face right now?
- 🚧 What's currently stopping them from reaching that vision?

## B.I.A.S. applied to your own communication

If a message lands wrong, reverse-engineer it with the same framework used on screens:

- 🙈 **Block** — a message that seems redundant, high-effort, or unrelated to the audience's vision gets filtered out.
- 💭 **Interpret** — craft the message with the audience's frame: what's familiar to them, what do they benefit from, what do they fear losing?
- ⏰ **Act** — remove options (small chunks of info at a time) and nudge effectively (similar industry examples, curiosity).
- 💾 **Store** — every good interaction makes the next one easier; communication compounds.

## Four tactics for presenting decisions

1. **Lead with a story** — stories rally people around the user instead of individual opinions, and generate new ideas.
2. **Use the right vocabulary** — it's hard to argue against a named psychological principle; "opinions" dissolve against science.
3. **Create feedback guardrails** — never show designs without saying specifically what feedback you want; it focuses the conversation and speeds decisions.
4. **Know how to answer feedback** — never react negatively to criticism:
   - 🌱 *Lead with a yes*: acknowledge and thank them for caring about the work.
   - 💫 *Repeat and empathize*: summarize what they said; rephrase it as a question that makes them elaborate; ask what feelings drive the statement (what are they afraid of?).
   - 🙏 *Assure*: confirm you also want the best solution — before you respond.

## Handling the three difficult archetypes

- **"I don't like it"** — transform *likes* into *works*: "I understand you don't like this — do you think it works? If not, why doesn't it work?" Steers from opinion to fact.
- **"Why change anything?"** — gut feeling isn't a foundation; bring user research (GEQs/SEQs) and data showing the gap, and base the proposed improvement on named psychological principles.
- **"Let's change everything"** — ask "what doesn't work right now?" first; include the psychology behind your decisions when presenting; and when the discussion becomes opinion tennis, test variants — all opinions are hypotheses, and hypotheses can be wrong.

## Pre-meeting checklist

- What's the goal of the review, and what do I want feedback on? (guardrails)
- What problem does this solution solve — is it a business problem?
- How does it currently affect users? (the story behind it)
- Why is this solution better than the alternatives?

If you can't answer one of these, go back to the drawing board before the meeting.

## Key sentences

Vocabulary that keeps discussion anchored in user psychology instead of taste:

- "How can we align the copy with the benefits…"
- "It facilitates the user's goal…"
- "How can we overcome this user's barrier…"
- "Users will block this because…"
- "It draws the user's attention…"
- "The framing isn't exactly aligned with…"
- "It emphasizes users' benefits…"
- "How can we reduce friction even more…"
- "Could we nudge people to…"

Finally: focus on what you can control — other people might also have great ideas, and openness generates better ones.
